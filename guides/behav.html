Behaviour

A mirror for how you trade, not for the market. Behaviour counts the habits that cost traders money (holding losers, widening stops, trading on tilt, entering with warning signs), asks you to imagine a trade’s failure before risky orders go out, and keeps score of what each warning has cost you. Three pages: Habits, Pre-mortem and Red flags, under one Behaviour score.

I

Orientation

What the tab measures, how it is organised, and the score that sits above every page.

01

What Behaviour is, and what it is not

Every number here comes from your own trades and decisions: when you closed, what you were holding, what the ticket knew at entry, what you said in a pre-mortem. Nothing on this tab reads the market for a signal.

Habits

Do you bank winners and keep losers (the disposition effect)? Do losses cluster after losses (tilt)? Did your wins come with sound risk control or without it?

Pre-mortem

Before a risky trade you imagine it has already failed and name why. Afterwards the tab compares what you expected with what actually happened, and how well your stated odds matched results.

Red flags

Every entry is checked against fourteen warnings. The scoreboard shows what trades with each warning won and lost, and lets you turn a warning into a rule.

Why it matters

Most retail losses do not come from a bad market view. They come from a few repeated habits: a stop moved away, a loser held because closing it hurts, a trade opened minutes after a loss. These habits are measurable, and what is measured can be changed one rule at a time.

Not a verdict on you

Every reading needs enough trades. Below the sample sizes in this guide (10, 20 and 30 trades) Behaviour says “not clear yet” or “may be chance” rather than guessing. A short run of bad luck is not a bad habit.

II

Habits

The first page: how you exit, how you react to losses, and whether your wins were earned.

05

Disposition effect: what is counted

The disposition effect is the tendency to sell winners too early and ride losers too long (Odean, 1998). The card measures it exactly the way Odean did: at every moment you choose to close something, it looks at everything you hold.

PGR (gains realised) = rg ÷ (rg + pg) · share of the winning positions you held that you chose to close
PLR (losses realised) = rl ÷ (rl + pl) · share of the losing positions you held that you chose to close
PGR − PLR = the needle of the gauge · above 0: winners go first
ratio = PGR ÷ PLR · “you realise gains 1.50× as readily as losses”

What counts as a decision

CloseCounted?Why
Closed by hand (button, ticket, partial close)yesAn in-the-moment decision.
Close all, close winners, close losers, close by symbolyes, onceThe whole batch is one decision, counted before any leg closes.
Stop loss, Take profit, Trailing stop, Time stopnoA plan made earlier, not a decision now.
Basket stop, Basket target, basket legsnoBasket legs are neither sold nor held in the count.
Only one position open (the one you close)noOdean counts only portfolios of two or more positions.
Every position closed is at exactly $0.00noNothing realised that is a gain or a loss.

The totals rg, rl, pg, pl are kept for all time. The last 300 decisions are also kept, only to draw the small line “PGR − PLR over your last N closes”.

06

Disposition: verdict, gauge and readouts

A gap between PGR and PLR can be chance. The card judges it with Odean’s t-statistic and gives one verdict, used everywhere (this card, its toast, the score, the Journal coach and the report).

SE = √( PGR(1 − PGR) ÷ (rg + pg) + PLR(1 − PLR) ÷ (rl + pl) )
t = (PGR − PLR) ÷ SE
If every gain was sold and no loss (or the reverse), SE is 0: the pooled two-proportion SE is used instead, with p = (rg + rl) ÷ all positions counted.
n (Voluntary closes) = rg + rl · positions you closed yourself that were a gain or a loss

Reading the gauge

  • Left zone (−0.5 to −0.1): you cut losers more readily; the app colours it as good.
  • Middle (±0.1): close to even.
  • Right zone (+0.1 to +0.5): you bank winners and keep losers; coloured as bad.
  • The number under it is PGR − PLR with its sign; “—” before both a gain and a loss have been held.

The zones are context, not the verdict. A needle in the right zone with a dashed line means the gap could still be chance.

Tilt guard and Pause

“Tilt” is trading to win back a loss. The guard watches two things: losing trades in a row and how far the account is down today. When either reaches your limit, it trips.

Settings (a sentence you complete)

ControlChoicesDefault
Guard onon / offon
after … losses in a row2 · 3 · 4 · 53
or a day down …1% · 2% · 3% · 5%3%
cool-down …5 · 15 · 30 · 60 min15 min

How the two triggers are computed

  • Streak: finished positions (not basket legs), newest first; each one with a net loss adds 1, the first one at $0.00 or better stops the count.
  • Day: the net of every close since 00:00 UTC (partial closes included, after commission), as a % of the balance at the start of the day: day % = today’s net ÷ (balance − today’s net) × 100. Armed when it is at or below −limit.
  • The guard trips once per closing trade: the cool-down starts, “Times triggered” goes up by one, and a toast says “Tilt guard on”. After the cool-down it stays armed (pre-mortem on every trade) as long as either condition holds.
Example

You start the UTC day with $10,000. Three closes lose $140, $100 and $80: the balance is $9,680, today’s net −$320. Day % = −320 ÷ (9,680 + 320) = −3.20%, and the streak is 3. Both reach the defaults; the card reads “Cooling down · 15:00” with “3 losing trades in a row”, and the ticket adds the line “Tilt guard: 3 losing trades in a row — cool-down 15:00 left.” A winner breaks the streak, but the day stays at or below −3% unless it recovers, so the guard stays armed for the rest of the UTC day.

Calm · “2 losses in a row · today −$120.00 (−1.20%)”The guard is on and not armed; the line shows how close you are to each trigger.
Times triggeredHow often the guard has tripped.
OverridesHow often you pressed “Override cool-down” (red when above 0). These count against Discipline in the score.
After-loss tradesTrades opened within 10 minutes of a losing close (the revenge red flag, chapter 15).
Step away on your own terms

The Pause 15 min, Pause 1 hour and Pause Rest of day buttons (the rest of the UTC day, at least 10 minutes) do the same as a cool-down, by your choice: until the pause ends every new trade gets a pre-mortem, with “Override pause” as the only way through. Resume now ends it. Set the triggers once while calm; changing them in the middle of a losing run defeats the point.

08

Process vs outcome

A good decision can lose and a bad one can win. Judging a trade by its P&L alone is outcome bias (Baron & Hershey, 1988). This card judges each of your last 60 trades by what was knowable at entry.

good process = a stop at entry (or an R recorded) and none of the flags No stop loss · Risk above 2% of equity · Costs large vs the stop
That is: a stop, risk ≤ 2% of equity, spread under 15% of the stop distance. Trend and chase warnings are not part of it: only risk control is judged.
luck share = Dumb luck ÷ (Deserved win + Dumb luck) × 100
Example: 6 ÷ (14 + 6) = 30% of your wins came from poor-process trades.

Under the table: “30% of your wins (n=20) came from poor-process trades — results you should not repeat on purpose. 12 good-process trades lost anyway — a loss with a sound plan is not a mistake.” The share is shown red above 40%. With fewer than 6 trades the card asks for a few more.

How to use it

Repeat what sits in the top row, win or lose. Dumb-luck wins are the dangerous ones: they reward exactly what will hurt later. Bad breaks need no fixing; poetic justice shows what to stop.

When a pre-mortem asks

A pre-mortem (Klein, 2007) asks you to imagine that a decision has already failed and explain why. Imagining the failure as a fact (“prospective hindsight”) helps people name more of the real reasons in advance.

Why the random-walk sentence is there

Price touching a level after you left is common even when nothing predicts it. The tab gives the chance that a market with no drift touches that level within 24 hours, from the reflection principle:

P(touch within 24h) = 2 × (1 − Φ( d ÷ (σ × √24) ))
d = distance from your exit to the level, as a fraction of the exit price; σ = the standard deviation of hourly log returns over the last complete 1-hour bars (up to 101, at least 15). Without enough history: “That can happen by chance alone”.
Example

EUR/USD stopped out at 1.0850; your target is 30 pips away (d = 0.0030 ÷ 1.0850 = 0.28%). With σ = 0.08% per hour, d ÷ (σ√24) = 0.71 and the touch chance is about 48%. In a busier hour with σ = 0.12% it is about 64%. One stop-out followed by the target is close to a coin flip, not proof that the stop was wrong.

13

Confidence calibration

“How likely is it to work?” turns every rated trade into a forecast. Calibration asks whether your 70% trades really win about 70% of the time. The measure is the Brier score (Brier, 1950) with Murphy’s decomposition.

Brier = mean of (stated probability − outcome)² · outcome 1 = net win, 0 = loss; lower is better
Brier = reliability − resolution + uncertainty
reliability: how far each stated level sits from what happened at it (lower is better) · resolution: how well your odds separate winners from losers (higher is better) · uncertainty = base rate × (1 − base rate), what your own win rate alone leaves
BSS (skill) = 1 − Brier ÷ uncertainty · 0 = no better than stating your own win rate every time; below 0 worse

Reading the dots

  • On the dashed line: you win what you say.
  • Whole interval below the level you stated: overconfident at that level (the app colours the dot red).
  • Whole interval above it: underconfident (caution colour).
  • Otherwise: within chance (green). Hover a dot in the app: “Said 70% · won 4 of 10 = 40% (17–69%)”.

The verdict line

  • Under 20 rated trades: “Need N more rated trades before judging …”.
  • Overconfident by N points. when your average stated % is above the whole 95% interval of your actual win rate.
  • Underconfident by N points when it is below it.
  • No clear miscalibration. otherwise.
Example

22 rated trades: 8 at 50% (4 won), 10 at 70% (4 won), 4 at 80% (2 won). Average stated 64.5%, actual 10 of 22 = 45% (27–65%). Brier 0.303 = reliability 0.057 − resolution 0.002 + uncertainty 0.248; BSS = 1 − 0.303 ÷ 0.248 = −0.22, so the score part is 50 + 50 × (−0.22) = 39. Yet the verdict is “No clear miscalibration”: 64.5% is just inside the interval’s top (65%). A 19-point gap over 22 trades is still within chance; the 70% level on its own is already outside its interval.

“Against the 1-hour trend” and “Chasing an extended move” are shown, stored and scored on the board, but the tab never suggests a rule for them, they do not count against Clean entries and they are not part of “good process”. On a market with no drift, being with or against a recent move carries no edge either way. Your own record on the board is what tells you whether they matter for you.

    When the tab suggests a rule

    A suggest button appears on a row when all of these hold; pressing it sets the rule to pre-mortem (toast “Rule added”, “flag → pre-mortem every time”):

    1. at least 10 trades carried the warning;
    2. it is not the trend or chase warning;
    3. their net is negative;
    4. it has no rule yet;
    5. their win rate is more than 10 points below the No warnings row (or there is no baseline yet).
    Example

    Stop inside normal noise: 14 trades, 4 won = 29% (12–55%), net −$410.00. No warnings: 30 trades, 16 won = 53% (36–70%). 29 is more than 10 points below 53, the net is negative and there are 10+ trades: suggest appears, and the score card links to it. The intervals still overlap, so the gap is not yet proof; the rule is cheap insurance while the evidence builds.

    Your trading hours

    Net P&L of your closed trades grouped by the UTC hour you opened them, as 24 bars: above the line hours that paid, below hours that cost; the tallest bar sets the scale. Hover a bar in the app: “14:00 UTC · 6 trades · −$230.00”.

    The line under it: “Worst hour 14:00 (−$700.00, n=9, may be chance) · best 09:00 (+$400.00, n=7, may be chance). A gap between hours is mostly noise until each hour has 30+ trades.” It needs at least two hours with two or more trades each.

    Read with care

    24 groups split a small record thin: with 40 trades the average hour has fewer than two. Treat a bad hour as a question (what do I trade then, and why?) until it has 30+ trades.

    Limits and caveats

    Small samples

    Every verdict has a minimum: 4 voluntary closes for a first reading and 10 with |t| ≥ 2 for a disposition verdict, 20 rated trades for calibration, 10 for suggestions and mood verdicts, 30 before “may be chance” goes away.

    Your answers matter

    Mood, confidence and “Did it fail for a reason you named?” are only as good as what you tick. Skipped answers leave parts of the tab empty.

    After the fact is not proof

    “Stopped out, then target” and “missed win” happen often by chance; the random-walk figure shows how often.

    Checks are simple

    The noise check uses the average range of 15 fifteen-minute bars; the trend check an EMA50 on 1-hour bars. They flag common mistakes; they do not judge a setup.

    Entry context needs the open

    Flags are recorded when a position opens. Trades from before entry checks existed, or without context, are left out of the scoreboard and Clean entries.

    Not advice

    Behaviour describes your own past decisions. Nothing here predicts the market or recommends a trade.

    Glossary

    Disposition effect
    Selling winners too early and holding losers too long (Shefrin & Statman; measured by Odean, 1998).
    PGR / PLR
    Proportion of gains realised / of losses realised: of the winning (losing) positions you held at a voluntary close, the share you closed.
    Paper gain / loss
    A position still open, in profit or in loss, at the moment you close something else.
    t-statistic
    The gap PGR − PLR divided by its standard error; |t| ≥ 2 means a gap this size is unlikely to be chance.
    Tilt
    Trading to win back losses, usually faster and bigger than planned.
    Cool-down
    A set time after the tilt guard trips during which new trades need a pre-mortem and an override.
    Pre-mortem
    Imagining a decision has already failed and naming why, before taking it (Klein, 2007).
    Prospective hindsight
    Treating a future outcome as certain; it helps people find more reasons for it (Mitchell, Russo & Pennington, 1989).
    Post-mortem
    The evidence gathered after the trade: flags, news, stop changes, and what price did in the next 24 hours.
    Red flag
    A warning sign checked automatically at entry.
    Rule
    What a red flag does: warn, force a pre-mortem, or block until you deliberately break it.
    R
    The money at risk to the stop at entry; a result in R is net P&L ÷ that risk.
    Outcome bias
    Judging a decision by its result instead of by what was knowable when it was made.
    Brier score
    Mean squared gap between stated probability and outcome (1 or 0); lower is better.
    Reliability · resolution · uncertainty
    Murphy’s parts of the Brier score: calibration error, separation of winners from losers, and what the base rate alone leaves.
    BSS
    Brier skill score, 1 − Brier ÷ uncertainty: skill over always stating your own win rate.
    Wilson interval
    A 95% range for a win rate that stays sensible with few trades.
    Random walk
    A price path with no drift; used here to say how often a level is touched by chance.
    Time stop
    A deadline set in the pre-mortem: at it, the trade is closed unless it is in profit.
    EMA50
    Exponential moving average over 50 bars; here on 1-hour bars.

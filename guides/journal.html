I

Orientation

What the Journal measures, what to read first, where everything is.

01

What the Journal is, and what it is not

The Journal turns the trades you closed into numbers you can act on. Every figure is computed from your own executions on this account: nothing is estimated, nothing is added.

A record, not a forecast

It describes what your trades did. A good record says your method worked on these trades; it does not promise the next ones.

Net, per position

Commission is taken off every trade, and the partial closes of one position are counted as one trade, so the numbers match your balance.

Honest about sample size

The win rate carries a 95% interval, and the Coach stays silent on a group until it has 10 trades, flagging it as possibly chance below 30.

Why it matters

Most traders remember their trades by feel. The Journal replaces feel with three questions you can answer every week: do my trades make money after costs (expectancy), do my wins pay for my losses (profit factor, average R), and how deep did my account fall on the way (max drawdown)?

II

The statistics

Every tile, the formula behind it, and how to read it.

04

One trade, one position

The account history keeps one row per close. If you close a position in two parts, it has two rows. The Journal merges the rows of one position (same id, instrument, side and open time) into one trade before it counts anything.

net of a row = P&L − commission share
net of a trade = Σ net of its rows
trade lots = Σ lots · exit, pips and R = lot-weighted across the parts
R is kept only when every part has one (every part had a stop at the open); otherwise the trade has no R.
CaseWhat the Journal does
Position closed in partsOne trade; Trade notes shows “2 parts” (or more) next to the symbol.
Partly closed, rest still openCounted already, with the closed parts only; marked “2 parts, still open”. Its P&L grows when the rest closes.
Negative balance protection creditNot a trade: it is left out of every statistic and the Coach, but it moves the equity curve (chapter 10).
CommissionPaid in full at the open; each close carries its share, so the trade’s net includes all of it.
Break-even trade (net exactly $0)Counts as a loss in the win rate, average loss and the Coach.

06

Win rate and its interval

The win rate is the share of trades that ended above zero after commission. The tile shows it large, and under it the 95% Wilson interval and the number of trades, e.g. 62% over (43–78%) · n=26.

p = wins ÷ n    z = 1.96
d = 1 + z² ÷ n
centre c = (p + z² ÷ 2n) ÷ d
half-width e = z · √( p(1 − p) ÷ n + z² ÷ 4n² ) ÷ d
interval = [c − e, c + e], kept within 0–100%; with no wins the low end is 0%, with no losses the high end is 100%
All three numbers are rounded to whole percent.

Worked example. 16 wins in 26 trades: p = 0.615, d = 1 + 3.8416 ÷ 26 = 1.148, c = (0.615 + 0.074) ÷ 1.148 = 0.601, e = 1.96 × √(0.00910 + 0.00142) ÷ 1.148 = 0.175. Interval 0.426 to 0.776: the tile reads 62% (43–78%) · n=26.

The rate alone says little

A high win rate with small wins and large losses still loses money; a 35% win rate with wins three times the losses makes money. Read the win rate together with the average win and loss (chapter 08).

07

Profit factor

gross wins = Σ net of the trades with net > 0
gross losses = − Σ net of the trades with net ≤ 0
profit factor = gross wins ÷ gross losses  (2 decimals; “∞” with wins and no losses; “—” before any trade)
Profit factorMeans
below 1.00Losses outweigh wins: ▼ net loss.
1.00Break-even after commission.
above 1.00Wins outweigh losses: ▲ net gain. How far above matters less than whether it holds on more trades.
∞No losing trade yet. Usually a sign of a short record, not of a perfect method.
08

Average win, average loss, expectancy

Avg win = gross wins ÷ number of wins  (shown +$)
Avg loss = − gross losses ÷ number of losses  (shown −$)
Expectancy = (gross wins − gross losses) ÷ trades = Net P&L ÷ trades
Equivalently: win rate × avg win + (1 − win rate) × avg loss.

Worked example. Wins of 120, 45, 210, 150, 75 and losses of −80, −100, −60, −90, −40 dollars. Avg win = 600 ÷ 5 = +$120.00; avg loss = −370 ÷ 5 = −$74.00; expectancy = 230 ÷ 10 = +$23.00 a trade. Check: 0.5 × 120 + 0.5 × (−74) = 23.

The break-even win rate

The Coach uses the same averages: when your average loss is more than 1.5× your average win, it says so, with the win rate you would need just to break even.

break-even win rate = avg loss ÷ (avg loss + avg win)  (both as positive amounts)
Example: avg loss $150, avg win $80 → 150 ÷ 230 = 65%. The Coach line: “Average loss ($150.00) is 1.9× your average win. You'd need a 65% win rate just to break even.”

09

Average R

R is a trade’s result measured in units of what it risked at its stop. A trade that risked $100 and made $150 is +1.5R; one stopped out at its original stop is about −1R.

risk at the open = |entry − stop| × lots × contract size, in USD  (only when the order had a stop)
R of a close = P&L of that close ÷ its share of the risk
R of a trade = lot-weighted across its parts (= total P&L ÷ total risk)
Avg R = mean of R over the trades that have one  (2 decimals, signed, e.g. +0.28R)
Trades opened without a stop have no R and are left out of Avg R; the tile shows “—” if none has one.
R is before commission

R is computed from the close’s P&L, which is before commission; the dollar tiles are after it. So Avg R × risk is a little higher than the expectancy: in the example of chapter 16 (risk $100, commission $5 a trade), Avg R is +0.28R ≈ $28 a trade, against an expectancy of $23. The $5 gap is the commission.

Avg RReads as
above 0On average a trade returned more than nothing per unit risked: ▲ positive, before costs.
around 0Wins and losses in R cancel: no edge before costs, a loss after them.
below 0▼ trades lose more of their risk than they win.
losses well beyond −1RLook in the History tab: stops moved away, or gaps and slippage through them.

R also feeds the Risk Lab’s Use my journal button (Strategy Lab › Risk Lab). It needs at least 5 trades with a stop; it fills the win rate and the reward : risk from those trades, and the risk per trade from the median of what your trades really risked at their open, as a % of the balance before each.

10

The equity curve and max drawdown

The curve is your balance after each closed trade, oldest on the left. It starts from what the balance was before the oldest trade the history still holds, and adds each trade’s net at its last close.

ElementWhat it is
LineOne point per closed trade, plus one per protection credit; 2 px, no fill.
Dashed lineThe starting balance, labelled “start $…”. Above it the record is up, below it down.
Highlighted point and boxThe last point, or the point nearest the mouse with a vertical guide. The box gives the balance and the change since the start, with a sign and “· trade n” while you hover.
AxesDollars on the left; on the bottom the times of the first, middle and last trade (month-day hours:minutes, UTC).
Empty“Your equity curve appears after your first closed trade.”
start = balance now + commission already paid on open positions − Σ net of every history row
curve = start, then + net of each trade in the order of its last close
max drawdown = max over the curve of (running peak − balance) ÷ running peak  (shown as −x.xx%)
Negative balance protection

If a fast market took the account below zero and the protection credit topped it back up, the closes just before that credit are drawn at $0: you never owed that money. So the curve and the drawdown never go past −100%.

Read the shape, not only the end

A curve that ends up but saw a 15% fall on the way was harder to trade than one that rose evenly to the same place. Max drawdown is the number that tells you whether you could have stayed with the method at your size.

Read the interval column first

Two groups whose intervals overlap widely are not yet different, however far apart their win rates look. In the example, EUR/USD (39–84%) and Impulse (9–53%) overlap from 39% to 53%: a hint, not a proof. Net and Avg tell you what each group did to the account.

IV

Using it

A routine, a full example to check against, and what the numbers cannot tell you.

14

A weekly review

Twenty minutes once a week, at the same time, before the new week starts:

  1. Notes
    Fill the gapsOpen Coach › Details. Give every losing trade of the week one line of note: what you saw, what you would change.
  2. Score
    Read the four numbersNet P&L, expectancy, profit factor, max drawdown. Write them down with the trade count, so you can compare weeks.
  3. Curve
    Find the worst stretchHover the deepest dip of the equity curve; read which trades caused it in the Trades table.
  4. Coach
    Take the costliest lineOf the Coach lines, pick the one costing most money (no stop loss, holding losers, re-entries). Ignore lines flagged “may be chance” as reasons to change your method.
  5. Groups
    Compare groupsIn By pair, By session and By side, look at Net and Avg; check whether the intervals overlap before deciding a group is better.
  6. Rule
    One changeWrite one rule for next week (for example: no new trade within 10 minutes of a loss). One rule, so next week’s numbers can show whether it helped.

16

Limits and pitfalls

Small samples

Under 30 trades almost every figure can be luck. The win rate interval shows it; the other tiles have no interval but are just as unsure.

Many groups, some look good by chance

With many pairs, four sessions and two sides, one group will look best even if all are equal. The Coach’s thresholds reduce, not remove, that risk.

The newest 500 closes

The history keeps 500 rows (closes, not trades). Older trades leave the statistics and the curve’s start moves.

Still-open remainders

A partly closed position already counts, with its closed parts only; its figures change when it closes.

R and dollars differ

R is before commission, dollar tiles after it; trades without a stop have no R, so Avg R can describe a different set of trades than the rest.

Past, not future

The Journal describes what happened. Markets change; a method that worked last month may not next month.

Not advice

The Journal and the Coach describe your own record. They are not a recommendation to trade any instrument or session.

Glossary

Net P&L
Profit or loss after commission (swap included in the P&L).
Trade
One position, from open to its last close, however many parts it was closed in.
Win rate
The share of trades with a net above zero.
Wilson interval
A range that holds the true win rate with 95% confidence, given the wins and trades so far; it narrows as trades accumulate.
Profit factor
Gross wins divided by gross losses.
Expectancy
The average net result of one trade.
R (R-multiple)
A trade’s result divided by the money it risked at its stop when opened.
Equity curve
The account balance after each closed trade, in order.
Drawdown
A fall of the balance from its highest point so far, as a percentage of that point.
Re-entry
A trade opened within 10 minutes of a losing close; how revenge trading starts.
Negative balance protection
A credit that brings a balance pushed below zero back to zero; not a trade.

01

The question

The question

“Would other exit rules have done better on your own closed trades? Your trades replayed with nine exit rules.”

“Most traders tune entries and ignore exits.” Exit lab replays your closed FX and CFD trades on the real price path, each in its own unit, and asks: with the same entries, what would other exit rules have earned? Use it to judge your exits, not your entries.

02

Trades and unit

ChoiceOptions · defaultEffect
TradesMy trades · N · Sample traderYour closed trades (one record per position, partial closes merged), the 200 most recent at most. The sample trader takes random entries with no edge, grabs small wins and lets losses hit the stop ($100 a trade): its numbers show costs only. New sample draws another.
UnitR = my stop · R = volatility1R = your initial stop distance, or 1.5 × the 15-minute volatility at entry (also used for trades without a stop). R = volatility mixes trades with different risks: compare rules as shape, not size.

A trade is left out when it is older than the loaded 1-minute history, has under 15 minutes of price after entry, or its entry or exit is not on the bar path within spread plus a tolerance. Exit lab needs 5 replayed trades; with fewer it offers Try it with a sample trader. The sample is not kept after a reload.

04

What you see

Total R gives every trade the same weight; P&L, in dollars with your commission, weights each by its size, so the two signs can differ. Max DD is the worst losing stretch you would have sat through. Exit efficiency is how much of the best open profit (MFE) your exits kept on trades that were ever in profit: under 30%, profits were often given back.

Worked example

The 24 trades, sorted by entry time, split into 12 older and 12 newer. On the older half the best rule is Trail 1R. On the newer half it makes +0.3 R against −1.4 R from your own exits, paired t 1.1. That is far from p below 0.05 ÷ 8 = 0.00625: No reliable difference. Target 3R’s +2.2 R in the table is hindsight.

06

Stop × target map

Rows: stop distance as a multiple of base R. Columns: target as a multiple of that stop (“none” = exit after 24h). Because each cell is counted in R of its own stop, a 2R stop, which risks twice as much, has its total halved: cells compare results at equal risk. A lone bright cell among dark ones is luck; a bright neighbourhood is more believable.

07

Where you lose, and the trades

Where you lose groups your trades by how they closed (Manual, Stop loss, Take profit, Trail, Break-even, Time stop, Stop-out), sorted from the type that lost the most; under 3 trades a type shows “too few (under 3)”. The trades table lists the 12 most recent: your result, Best seen (MFE) in R, the best rule on that one trade, and Open chart, which switches the symbol and zooms the chart onto the trade.

Use on Target 1R/2R/3R, Trail 1R or Break-even + 3R sets the order ticket to that rule, with 1R = your ticket stop (or the volatility stop on the open instrument when the ticket has none); split targets are switched off so the ticket matches the rule. With no stop to build on: “Ticket unchanged … set a stop in the ticket first.” The ticket has no exit by time, so time stops are by hand.

Start with the sample

Learn the page on the sample trader; switch to your trades when at least 5 can be replayed. Fewer than about 30 makes every comparison loose.

Believe the walk-forward

Hindsight always finds a winner. Only a rule that also wins on the newer trades is worth switching to.

Fix the habit first

The exit type that lost the most is where to look first. A large gap between Best seen and Yours means profit was available and given back.

Limits

Counterfactual exits pay the normal spread but no slippage, and stops fill exactly at the level unless the price gapped through it. Choosing the best of eight rules after the fact overstates its edge; test it forward before adopting it. On a pure random walk, stops cannot add expected return — they only help when prices trend.

Observatory

Twelve research tools that look at the currency market in ways a price chart cannot: which pairs move together, whether recent moves had memory or were noise, how busy the market is by the hour, which currency is doing the moving, whether a signal has ever paid, and whether other exits would have served your own trades better. Each tool answers one question and tests its answer against chance. This page covers what the tools share: how a view is laid out, which tool answers which question, the statistics behind them and how to chain them. Each tool has its own guide (chapter 06).

Orientation

What the Observatory is, how every view is laid out, and which tool to open for which question.

01

What the Observatory is, and what it is not

The Observatory is a set of research tools in the Research workspace. Each entry in its list names a tool and the one question it answers. Three properties decide how you should use them.

Currencies first

The Observatory, Edge check and Baskets are built on the eight G8 currencies and their 16 pairs. CFDs are not part of them. A few tools also run on other instruments (see the table below).

Tested against chance

Every tool compares what it measures with what luck alone would produce: a noise band, shuffled copies, a Poisson band, a confidence interval, or a correction for testing many things at once.

Descriptive

The tools describe the recent window. “Persistent”, “busier than usual” or “EUR-led” say what happened, not what comes next. Only the Range cone gives odds about the future, and it reports how well those odds held last week.

Statistics in one page

The handful of ideas the tools use to tell a real pattern from luck. Read it once; each tool chapter refers back here.

05

Statistics in one page

Two questions run through every tool: how strong is what I see, and could chance alone have produced it? The first is a measurement (ρ, Hurst, an average). The second compares that measurement with its noise: a standard error, a band from randomised copies, or a correction for testing many things at once.

Correlation ρ and correlation distance

ρ (Pearson correlation) measures how two series of returns go up and down together at the same steps, from −1 (always opposite) through 0 (unrelated) to +1 (move as one). It says nothing about the size of the moves, only how they line up.

The Constellation turns ρ into a correlation distance d = √(2(1−|ρ|)): 0 for pairs that move as one, about 1.41 for unrelated ones. Because it uses |ρ|, a strong inverse link is also “close”: buying one and selling the other is the same trade.

Standard error and the t-statistic

An average from a small sample wobbles. Its standard error is the size of that wobble: SE = sd ÷ √n. Four times as many cases halve it.

The t-statistic t = average ÷ SE counts how many wobbles the average is away from zero. If there is nothing behind a result, |t| lands beyond 2 only about one time in twenty. That is why tools use “t > 2” as the first bar, and why they never stop there: with many tests, one in twenty is a lot of lucky passes.

A 95% interval is roughly the average ± 1.96 SE. Intrinsic time reports the average overshoot this way.

Fisher z: is this ρ different from normal?

ρ is squeezed near ±1, so a change from 0.80 to 0.74 is bigger than it looks. The Fisher transform z = atanh(ρ) stretches it onto a scale where the noise is about 1 ÷ √(n − 3). The Constellation compares a link’s ρ now with its ρ over the whole history kept, in standard errors of the difference, and widens that error by a factor k (never below 1) measured on the market’s own history.

Worked example

Normal ρ 0.80 over 9,997 returns; now 0.74 over 1,437 (the 1D window). z: 1.099 → 0.951, Δz = 0.148. SE = k × √(1/1434 − 1/9994) = 1.5 × 0.0244 = 0.0366. Δz ÷ SE = 4.0: beyond 3, so the link is marked broke 0.80 → 0.74. Had it been 0.77 now: 2.1 standard errors, not marked.

Hit rates, the Wilson interval and Bonferroni

“Right 29 times out of 40” is 72.5%, but 40 tries is a small sample. The Wilson interval gives the range where the true hit rate lies; if it contains 50%, the method is no better than a coin.

When a tool offers many settings, one of them will look good by luck. Bonferroni widens every interval as if all settings were tried: Déjà vu offers 27 settings, so it uses z = 3.11 (a two-sided 5% ÷ 27) instead of 1.96.

Counts and the Poisson band

If events arrive at an average pace, the count in a fixed window still varies. For a pace that predicts λ events, the Poisson distribution gives the range a window lands in 19 times in 20 by chance. Intrinsic time uses it for its heartbeat: at 3.2 events per hour, 6 hours predict λ = 19.2, and anything from 11 to 28 events is normal variation; 31 is busier than chance alone gives.

Noise bands: randomised copies and shuffles

Instead of a textbook formula, several tools build their own reference from your data:

  • Sign-randomised copies (Regime map): each return keeps its size and its time of day, but its sign is flipped at random. Busy and quiet hours stay; any memory of direction is gone. 160 copies, and the band holds 90% of them (5th to 95th percentile).
  • Shuffled copies (Recurrence, Baskets’ stretch, Edge check’s null rates): the same returns in random order: same sizes, no time structure.

A reading outside the band is unusual for noise with the same sizes and rhythm. With 16 pairs and a 90% band, one or two pairs fall outside by chance alone at any moment.

Many tests at once: Benjamini–Hochberg and Bonferroni

Test 80 signal–pair combinations with nothing behind any of them, and about four will pass |t| > 2. Two corrections stop those from being called discoveries:

  • Bonferroni: divide the threshold by the number of tests. Strict and simple. Déjà vu (27 settings) and Exit lab (8 rules: p below 0.05 ÷ 8) use it.
  • Benjamini–Hochberg (false discovery rate): sort the p-values, find the largest rank i whose p ≤ i ÷ N × q, and keep the first i. It keeps the share of lucky passes among the survivors under q. Edge check uses q = 10%.

The p-value is the chance of a result at least this strong if nothing were behind it.

Walk-forward and conformal bands: test on what was not used

A conformal band widens or narrows a model’s band by how often it missed in the past, so that its stated odds (68%, 95%) hold as a past frequency. It is not a guarantee.

Memory and randomness: Hurst and permutation entropy

Hurst exponent

The memory of the price path in the window. Sums of k returns grow like kH: 0.5 is a random walk, above its moves ran on more than chance, below they were undone more than chance. It describes the window, not the future.

Permutation entropy

How random the order of the moves is. Take 4 consecutive returns, note their up/down ranking (24 possible patterns) and measure how evenly the patterns occur. Near 1 = noise; lower = some patterns repeat. Short samples of pure noise sit a little below 1.

Epps effect

On real FX data, correlations measured on very short returns come out weaker, because pairs are not quoted at the same instants. The Constellation shows mean |ρ| at 1, 5 and 15 minutes so you can see whether the interval matters.

07

Workflows that chain tools

Before adding a position

  1. Overlap
    Constellation · 1D, then 4HOpen the pair. Is a neighbour already in your book? Does Your positions say concentrated risk? If so, the new trade is the same bet: size them as one.
  2. Reason
    Drivers · 4HWhich currency moved it? If the other currency is the driver, your reason for the trade may be the wrong one. Check the cleaner expression.
  3. Character
    Regime mapPersistent, inside the band or anti-persistent over the window? Match the tactic: trend-following is better served by a persistent window, fading by an anti-persistent one.
  4. Timing
    Volatility clockIs activity about to rise? Next two hours above 1.2×: give stops room. How many next-hour 1σ is your stop?
  5. Levels
    Range cone · your holding timeRead the promise check, then the levels: stop outside 68%, target not beyond 95%, and the Touched odds of both. Set ticket stop and target from the cone if you agree.
  6. Watch
    Intrinsic timeAlert at reversal on the δ you trade: if it fires, the current leg is over by this definition.
  7. Decide
    TicketReview the size, stop, target and costs; nothing in the Observatory sends an order.

Testing an idea

  1. Record
    Edge check · the horizon you hold forHas the signal paid on this pair? Only Robust counts. Compare the passes with the number expected by luck.
  2. Analogues
    Déjà vuDid shapes like the current one ever predict anything here? Read the verdict before the fan. Change one setting and see if the answer holds.
  3. Size
    Range coneEven an edge says nothing about distance: use the cone for stop and target, and the analogues’ spread as a second reference.
  4. Forward
    Trade small, write it downA survivor is a hypothesis. The test that counts is on data that did not exist when you chose it.

After a week of trading

  1. Exits
    Exit lab · R = my stopVerdict first, then the walk-forward card. No reliable difference: keep your exits.
  2. Habits
    Where you lose · Exit efficiencyWhich exit type lost the most? Under 30% efficiency, profits were often given back.
  3. Map
    Stop × target mapIs there a broad good area, or one bright cell? Prefer the area.
  4. Apply
    Use, then test forwardOnly a rule that passed the walk-forward is worth loading into the ticket with Use; trade it forward before adopting it.
  5. Book
    Constellation · 3DDid your positions carry the same risk all week? Read Your currency exposure.

09

Limits and pitfalls

One week of history

The terminal keeps a week of 1-minute bars. Every measure, band and record lives inside it; a week is one sample of market conditions.

Description, not forecast

Persistent, busier, EUR-led, Robust: each says what happened in the window. Only the Range cone gives odds, and it reports how they held.

Many tests, some luck

With 16 pairs, 120 links or 80 signal–pair tests, some results clear any bar by chance. That is why the tools count expected luck.

Pairs share currencies

Five pairs carry the yen, seven the dollar. Results on several pairs are rarely independent evidence.

Closed markets

A reading taken before a close or a weekend describes the past session. Check “as of” before acting.

Prices only

No news, positioning or order flow. A release can override every number on the page.

The tools never trade for you

Buttons such as Alert at reversal, Set ticket stop, Use, Trade <pair> instead or Open chart fill an alert or the ticket and send nothing. Only the Baskets Trade view sends orders, and only when you press its Buy or Sell button.

Glossary

95% CI
The range where the true hit rate lies with 95% confidence. If it contains 50%, the method is no better than a coin.
Analogues
Past stretches, on any of the 16 pairs, whose shape is closest to the last bars of this pair.
Anti-persistent
Over the window, at scales of 1 to 32 minutes, moves were undone more often than chance would undo them (Hurst below the noise band). A description of the past window, not a forecast.
Basis point (bp)
0.01%. 25 bp = 0.25%.
Basket
In Drivers: an equal-weighted group of all eight currencies, the reference each currency is measured against. In Baskets: a group of currencies, weighted, traded as one index through its pair legs.
Benjamini–Hochberg
A correction for testing many signals at once, so that few of the “significant” ones are flukes.
Bonferroni
Dividing the significance threshold by the number of tests or settings tried: 27 for Déjà vu, 8 rules for Exit lab.
Coastline
The length of the zigzag: every leg from extreme to extreme added up, in % of price. How far the price travelled, whatever the net move.
Conformal band
The cone widened or narrowed by how often it missed last week; its odds are a past frequency, not a guarantee.
Correlation distance
d = √(2(1−|ρ|)): 0 for pairs that move as one, about 1.4 for unrelated ones.
δ
The reversal size that counts as an event, in % of price.
Determinism
Share of recurrences that line up into diagonals: sequences of states that repeat. Compared with the same data shuffled.
Directional change
Price has reversed by δ from its last extreme: a new leg is confirmed.
Efficiency ratio
Net move divided by the whole path travelled: 100% = a straight line, near 0% = back and forth.
EMA20 · EMA50
Exponential moving averages of the last 20 and 50 bars, recent bars weighing more.
Epps effect
Correlations measured on very short returns come out weaker, because pairs are not quoted at the same instants.
False discovery rate
With many signals tested, some pass by luck. The Benjamini–Hochberg correction keeps the share of lucky passes under the stated rate.
Fisher z
atanh(ρ): a rescaling of ρ on which sampling noise is about 1 ÷ √(n − 3), used to compare a link now with its normal.
Heartbeat
Events in the last 6 hours of trading against the average pace: above 1 busier than usual. Highlighted only outside the range chance alone gives (a Poisson count at the average pace).
Horizon chart
A line folded into coloured bands: darker = further from the start of the window. 16 pairs fit in the space of one chart.
Hurst exponent
The memory of the price path in the window. Above 0.5 its moves ran on more than chance, below 0.5 they were undone more than chance, 0.5 is a random walk. It describes the window; it is not a forecast.
Intrinsic time
Time counted in reversals of δ instead of minutes: a busy hour holds many steps, a quiet one few.
Laminarity
Share of recurrences in vertical blocks: the market stuck in one state (consolidation).
Legs
Drivers: the two currencies of the pair, each measured against the basket of all eight. Baskets: the pair trades that together make up the basket position. Intrinsic time: the moves between two confirmed reversals.
MAE · MFE
Maximum adverse / favourable excursion: the worst open loss / the best open profit while the trade was held.
Max DD
Maximum drawdown: the deepest fall from a peak of the running total.
Minimum spanning tree
The fewest, strongest links that still connect every pair: 15 links for 16 pairs.
Noise band
Where the same returns land with their signs randomised (same sizes, same busy and quiet hours, no memory of direction): 90% of those copies fall inside it.
Overshoot
How far price kept going after a reversal was confirmed, in multiples of δ. Research finds about 1× δ on average.
p-value
The chance of a result at least this strong if nothing were behind it.
Permutation entropy
How random the order of the moves is: near 1 = noise, lower = some up-down patterns repeated in the window (Bandt & Pompe). Short samples of pure noise sit a little below 1.
Persistent
Over the window, at scales of 1 to 32 minutes, moves ran on more often than chance would carry them (Hurst above the noise band). A description, not a forecast.
Poisson band
The range of counts a window lands in 19 times in 20 when events arrive at a steady average pace.
Proxy
The single pair that tracks the basket most closely.
R
The risk of a trade, entry to stop. Results in R make trades of different sizes comparable.
Recurrence · recurrence rate
Two moments whose states are closer than the threshold: the market looked the same at both. The threshold is set so that the closest 10% of all pairs of states count.
ρ
From −1 to +1. +1: the two move as one; −1: always opposite; 0: unrelated.
RSI(14)
Relative strength index, 0–100: average up-moves against down-moves over 14 bars.
Shuffled
The same returns in random order: same sizes, no time structure. The reference for “is there structure here”.
σ
Standard deviation: the typical size of a move. 2σ is an unusually large distance.
Standard error
The typical wobble of an average from sample to sample: sd ÷ √n.
States
Three consecutive returns, divided by their usual size: the shape and relative size of a short stretch, not the price.
t-statistic
An average divided by its standard error. Beyond ±2 a single test would rarely be chance.
Walk-forward
The rule is chosen on older trades and checked on newer ones it never saw: a fairer test than picking the best in hindsight.
Wilson interval
A confidence interval for a hit rate that stays sensible on small samples; Déjà vu widens it to z = 3.11 for its 27 settings.
Win rate
Share of trades that closed in profit.

Correlation

A matrix of how alike every pair of instruments has moved: the same trade, the same trade in reverse, or unrelated. Use it to see whether a new position is really new, whether two positions add up or offset each other, and which instrument spreads your risk best.

I

Orientation

What the matrix measures, what to read first, where everything is.

01

What the matrix is, and what it is not

Every cell holds one number, ρ (rho), the correlation of two instruments’ returns over the window you choose. It answers one question: when one moved, did the other tend to move the same way, the opposite way, or neither?

About direction, not size

ρ says whether two instruments moved together. It does not say by how much: a pair with ρ 0.9 can still move twice as far as its partner.

About the past window

It describes the last 4 hours, day or 3 days of trading. Correlations shift, sometimes quickly around news.

About risk, not signals

It tells you how positions combine. It does not say which way to trade, and it is not a cause: two pairs move together because they share a currency or a driver.

Why it matters

Three positions on pairs with ρ near +1, held the same way, are one trade three times over: their stops tend to be hit together. The matrix shows that before you place the third order.

II

Reading it

What a value of ρ means, how to read the grid, and what the summary cards tell you.

04

What ρ means

ρ runs from −1 to +1. The words the matrix uses in its tooltips come from fixed bands of |ρ|:

|ρ|Tooltip saysIn practice
≥ 0.7, ρ > 0Strongly correlated: close to the same tradeBuying both doubles one bet.
≥ 0.7, ρ < 0Strongly inversely correlated: buying one and selling the other is close to the same tradeBuying both is close to flat; buy one and sell the other doubles the bet.
0.4 – 0.7, ρ > 0Moderately correlated: they share part of their movesRelated, not interchangeable.
0.4 – 0.7, ρ < 0Moderately inversely correlatedA partial hedge when held the same way.
0.2 – 0.4Weakly relatedMostly separate stories.
< 0.2Practically unrelated: a good diversifier against each otherSpreads risk.
Where the sign comes from in FX

Most strong links come from a shared currency. EUR/USD and GBP/USD both have USD as quote: a dollar move pushes both the same way (ρ > 0). EUR/USD and USD/CHF have USD on opposite sides: the same dollar move pushes them opposite ways (ρ < 0).

06

The three summary cards

Strongest links

The five pairs with the largest |ρ| in the matrix, sign shown. These are the places where two names hide one trade. Hover a link for its words.

Most independent

The instrument with the lowest average |ρ| against all the others, shown as “avg |ρ| 0.18”. The best diversifier against the rest of this set, on this window.

Your positions

Held instruments that are in the matrix. Any two of them beyond |ρ| 0.7 are listed with a tag: adding up or offsetting. Otherwise: “N held · no pair of them beyond |ρ| 0.7: risk is spread.”

Adding up or offsetting

Example

You bought EUR/USD and bought GBP/USD; on 1D their ρ is 0.72. Same side, ρ above 0.7: the card shows EUR/USD–GBP/USD 0.72 adding up. Size them as one trade: their stops are likely to be hit together.

08

How ρ is computed

SetWhich minutes
G8The last 240 / 1,440 / 4,320 minutes of trading that both pairs share (4H / 1D / 3D), matched minute by minute. A weekend is skipped, not counted, so 1D means a day of trading even on Monday morning.
YoursThe window of clock time (4 h, 1 day, 3 days) ending at the last minute both instruments traded. A market closed now (a stock at night, FX at the weekend) is read on its last session; the tooltip then says “up to time UTC, when the earlier of the two last traded”.
15m / 1h returnsOnly the common minutes on the clock’s 15-minute or hourly marks, so each return spans one full step.
Matched by time

Live quotes do not arrive in every minute for every pair: each one skips different quiet minutes. Pairing returns by their time, not by their position in each series, keeps the comparison honest; a misaligned pairing pulls every ρ towards zero.

The Epps effect

On very short returns, correlations come out weaker than they really are, because quotes do not update at the same instant. If 1m and 15m differ a lot for a pair, trust the 15m value for decisions held over hours.

09

Cluster order

Cluster reorders rows and columns so that instruments which move alike sit next to each other. Correlated groups then appear as blocks along the diagonal, and each block is, in effect, one trade.

How: each pair of instruments gets a distance d = √(2(1 − |ρ|)) (0 = move as one, 1.41 = unrelated). The shortest links that connect everything form a tree; the order walks that tree from the open instrument, nearest neighbour first.

Because the distance uses |ρ|, a strong inverse link (blue) also counts as close: the same trade in reverse sits next to its partner.

10

How sure is a ρ?

A correlation measured on a sample wobbles. For two instruments that are truly unrelated, about 95% of measured values fall within ±1.96 / √n of zero, where n is the number of returns:

Window · returnsn (about)Unrelated pairs still show |ρ| up to

1D · 1m1,4400.05
3D · 1m4,3200.03

So on 4H, a ρ of 0.10 says nothing; on 3D with hourly returns, even 0.20 may be chance. With 120 cells in the G8 matrix, a few will look notable by luck alone on any window.

The cross-check

Hover the cell: the tooltip lists the same pair on the other windows. A link above 0.5 on 4H, 1D and 3D is real structure; one that appears only on 4H is a passing coincidence until proven otherwise.

IV

Using it

Turning ρ into decisions about size, hedges and diversification.

11

Correlation and your risk

Two positions of the same size and volatility do not add their risk linearly. Their combined swing is √(2 + 2ρ′) times one position, where ρ′ = ρ if you hold them the same way and −ρ if you hold them opposite ways. Try it:

−1−0.50+0.5+1
1.84×adding up
Two positions, as many units of risk as1.84If they were unrelated (ρ 0)1.41If they were one trade (ρ′ = 1)2.00

Assumes equal size and equal volatility; it shows how much of the second position is really new risk. The same logic grows with every extra correlated position.

12

What to do with it

Before a new trade

Find its row. Strong orange against something you hold the same way, or strong blue against something you hold opposite, means the trade is not new: it enlarges what you already have.

Size correlated trades as one

If two positions add up (Fig. 4), split the risk you would put on one trade between them, instead of risking the full amount on each.

Diversify deliberately

Use the Most independent card, or a pale row, to add a position that shares little with your book on this window.

Check a hedge

A hedge only works while the link holds. If an “offsetting” pair’s ρ has fallen on 4H, the hedge is weaker than you think.

Pick the better vehicle

Two pairs with ρ 0.9 express the same view: trade the one with the cleaner setup or the lower spread, not both.

Beyond FX

Switch to Yours to see how your stocks, indices, commodities and crypto relate to your FX pairs, on the minutes they actually share.

A routine before adding a position

  1. Look
    Your positions cardAnything already adding up? Deal with that first.
  2. Look
    The new instrument’s rowDeep cells against held instruments, with the sign of how you hold them.
  3. Check
    Other windowsHover the cell: does the link hold on 4H, 1D and 3D?
  4. Decide

14

Limits and pitfalls

Correlation changes

Links tighten in stress and loosen in calm. Yesterday’s hedge can fail on the day you need it.

Not size, not cause

ρ is about direction only. Two pairs can be highly correlated and one can still move twice as far.

Sample noise

Small values on short windows are mostly chance (chapter 10). Read deep colours, not decimals.

Extreme days

One big move can dominate a short window; compare with 3D before concluding.

Not a signal

The matrix says how positions combine. It never says which way a market will go; nothing here is financial advice.

16

Glossary

ρ (correlation)
From −1 to +1: +1 the two move as one, −1 always opposite, 0 unrelated. Pearson correlation of returns.
Return
The change of price over one step, here the log return ln(close now ÷ close one step earlier).
Window
How much history each ρ is measured on: 4 hours, 1 day or 3 days.
Common minutes
Minutes in which both instruments have a bar; only these are compared.
Diversifier
A position that does not move with the others, so it lowers the risk of the whole.
Hedge
A position expected to gain when another loses, offsetting part of its risk.
Epps effect
Correlations measured on very short returns come out weaker, because quotes do not update at the same instants.
Cluster order
Rows and columns reordered so instruments that move alike sit together and groups show as blocks.
G8 currencies
USD, EUR, JPY, GBP, CHF, AUD, CAD, NZD; the 16 pairs of the G8 set are pairs of them.

Guide · Strategy Lab

Risk Lab

A Monte Carlo stress test of a trading rule. You give it a win rate, a reward : risk and a risk per trade; it plays 1,000 random sequences of wins and losses with those odds and shows the spread of results: the typical outcome, bad and good luck, how deep the drawdowns get and how often the account halves. Use it to pick a risk per trade whose bad days you could live through.

I

Orientation

What the Lab answers, what to read first, where everything is.

01

What the Risk Lab is, and what it is not

The same rule, with the same odds, can give very different accounts depending only on the order in which wins and losses arrive. The Risk Lab shows that spread. It answers: with these odds and this risk per trade, what range of results should I expect, and how bad can the road get?

Many futures, one rule

Each of the 1,000 paths is one possible order of wins and losses. Their spread is the part of the result that is luck.

Risk, not edge

Win rate and reward : risk set the edge. Risk per trade does not change the edge; it changes how far the account swings around it.

No market data

The Lab does not read prices. It is pure arithmetic on the three numbers you give it, so it says nothing about any instrument or about the future market.

Why it matters

A strategy with a real edge can still halve an account if the risk per trade is too high, and a rule with no edge can look good for a while by luck. Seeing 1,000 versions side by side separates the two questions: is there an edge? (expectancy) and can I survive the swings? (drawdown and ruin).

02

The Lab in one minute

Fill the odds

Type a win rate and a reward : risk, or press Use my journal to take them from your closed trades.

Set the risk

Risk per trade %: the share of equity each stop costs. Trades: how many you plan to take.

Run 1,000 paths

The Lab plays 1,000 random sequences with those odds.

Read the tail

Look first at the 5th percentile, the median max drawdown and the risk of ruin, not at the median.

Adjust

Lower the risk until the bad cases are ones you could stand; then run again.

II

Inputs and model

What each field means, what the Lab does with it, and how to fill it from your own record.

04

The four inputs

FieldDefaultAcceptedMeaning
Win rate %451 – 99The share of trades that win. Each trade is a win with this probability, independently of the others.
Reward : risk2≥ 0.1How many times the risk a win pays. Every win pays exactly this; every loss costs exactly 1 risk (1R).

A value outside the accepted range is pulled to the nearest limit before the run, without a message: type 0.01 in Risk per trade and the Lab uses 0.05%; type 5,000 trades and it uses 2,000. An empty field counts as 0, so it becomes the lower limit.

Reward : risk is an average

Real trades do not all win the same amount. Use the average win ÷ average loss in R, which is exactly what Use my journal computes. Teach me also advises lowering it for costs: spread and slippage cut every win.

05

How one path is built

Every path starts at equity 1 (100% of the account) and plays the trades one by one. For each trade the Lab draws a random number: below the win rate it is a win, otherwise a loss.

  1. Repeat for every trade of the path (the Trades field).
  2. After each trade, update the peak (highest equity so far, starting at 1), the max drawdown (largest fall from a peak, as a fraction of that peak), and a ruin flag set if equity is ever below 0.5.
  3. Repeat for 1,000 paths. Each run uses new random numbers.
  4. Sort the 1,000 final equities, the 1,000 max drawdowns, and at every trade the 1,000 equities at that trade; read the percentiles from the sorted lists (chapter 8).
What the model assumes

Every trade is independent of the last, the odds never change, every win pays exactly the reward : risk and every loss costs exactly the risk. There are no costs, no gaps through stops and no streaky behaviour beyond what chance produces. Chapter 18 explains what that leaves out.

After a fill, a message confirms the numbers used, for example “Loaded journal stats · 20 trades with a stop · 45% win · 2.00 R:R · risk 1% (median of 20 trades)”.

One outlier moves reward : risk

The risk per trade is the median, so one odd trade does not move it, but reward : risk is an average: a single winner with a very small stop (R of +50 or more) can push it far above what you normally get. If the filled reward : risk looks too good, type the value you usually aim for and run again.

How many trades count

Five trades is the minimum the button accepts, not enough to trust. A win rate from 20 trades can easily be 15 points away from the true one. Run the Lab with your journal numbers and again with a win rate 5–10 points lower: if the lower case is unacceptable, your current risk is too high for what you actually know.

III

Reading the outcomes

Every figure: how it is computed, how to read it, what to do with it.

07

Expectancy

The average result of one trade, in R (multiples of the risk). It is computed directly from the inputs, not from the paths, so it is the same on every run.

expectancy = win rate × R:R − (1 − win rate)
shown as “+0.35R / trade”. Above 0: the rule has an edge on paper. At 0 or below: it does not, whatever the risk per trade.

Break-even win rates

R:RBreak-evenAt 45% win

325.0%+0.80R

At 45% and 1:1: 0.45 × 1 − 0.55 = −0.10R. At 45% and 2:1: 0.45 × 2 − 0.55 = +0.35R: over 200 trades that is +70R on paper.

Expectancy is the edge; risk only scales it

No risk per trade turns a negative expectancy into a positive one. If the expectancy is at or below zero, the useful question is how to improve the rule, not how much to risk.

08

Median, 5th and 95th percentile

The 1,000 final equities, sorted from worst to best, are read at three places and shown as a % change from the start:

FigureRead from the sorted 1,000Read it as
Median resultthe 500thThe typical outcome: half the paths end below, half above.
5th pctthe 50th from the bottomBad luck: 1 path in 20 ends worse than this.
95th pctthe 950thGood luck: 1 path in 20 ends better than this.

Exactly: a percentile f is the value at position ⌊f × 999⌋ of the sorted list (counting from 0), with no interpolation.

Why the median, not the average

With compounding, a few very lucky paths end enormously high and pull the average up. The median is not moved by them: it is what a typical trader with these odds ends with. The gap between the median and the 95th percentile shows how much of the upside depends on luck.

Plan on the 5th percentile

Teach me puts it plainly: decide on the 5th percentile and the drawdown odds, not on the median. If the 5th percentile is a loss you could not accept over this many trades, the risk is too high or the edge too thin.

Run to run

Each run draws new random numbers, so the figures move a little between runs. With 1,000 paths a percentage like P(DD > 20%) near 10% typically moves by about ±1 point; the median result by a few %. If two settings differ by less than that, run each a few times before concluding. Chapter 17 gives the exact values for the final-equity figures, without the noise.

The share of the 1,000 paths whose final equity is above the start (strictly above 1). It answers: after this many trades, how often am I up at all?

Two lessons. A small edge needs many trades before it shows reliably. And a rule with exactly zero expectancy ends up less than half the time, not half: compounding a gain of x and a loss of x leaves you below where you started (1.01 × 0.99 = 0.9999). This drag grows with the risk per trade (chapter 14).

Profitable is not safe

At 45%, 2:1 and 20% risk per trade, about 93% of paths still end profitable, yet more than half of them fall below half the account on the way (chapter 11). Read this figure together with the drawdown and ruin figures, never alone.

10

Drawdown: Median max DD and P(DD > 20%)

A drawdown is a fall from the highest equity reached so far. The max drawdown of a path is its deepest one: the largest (peak − equity) ÷ peak at any trade. The peak starts at the starting equity.

FigureComputed asRead it as
Median max DDThe median of the 1,000 max drawdowns, shown negative (−9.1%).The deepest fall a typical path goes through. Half the paths see worse.
P(DD > 20%)The share of paths whose max drawdown is above 20% at any point.How often you would see the account at least a fifth below its best.

Why drawdown is the figure that decides

Losses and gains are not symmetric: after a fall, the gain needed to get back is larger than the fall.

Name your limit first

Decide the drawdown you would stop trading at, or the one a challenge or your own nerves allow (often 10% or 20%). Then lower the risk until the Median max DD sits well inside it and P(DD > 20%) is small.

11

Risk of ruin (−50%)

The share of paths in which equity falls below half the starting balance at any trade. A path that touches −50% and later recovers still counts as ruined: the Lab treats losing half as the end of the road, because few traders keep going after it and the climb back needs +100%.

risk of ruin = (paths with equity < 0.5 at some trade) ÷ 1,000
shown as a %; 0.0% means none of the 1,000 paths got there in this run, not that it is impossible.

How many straight losses reach −50%

Each loss multiplies equity by (1 − risk). After k losses in a row the account is at (1 − risk)k.

Ruin rarely comes from one unbroken streak; it comes from a bad stretch: more losses than usual over a few dozen trades. The Lab counts all of those, which is why it can show a ruin risk well before a streak of 69 losses is plausible.

Any figure above 0 deserves attention

At 45%, 2:1 over 200 trades, a typical run shows about 1.5% ruin at 5% risk, 16% at 10% and 53% at 20%, with a positive edge in every case. The edge is the same; only the risk changed.

The equity fan

The chart on the right of the Lab. Horizontally: trades, from “trade 0” to the last trade. Vertically: equity as a % change from the start, on a logarithmic scale, so +100% and −50% are the same distance from 0% (both are one doubling).

Width = luck

A wide fan means the result depends more on the order of wins and losses than on the edge. Teach me: lower the risk per trade.

The lower edge

Where the 5% line sits below 0% and for how long: how long a bad-luck trader stays under water.

The samples

Even paths that end high zig-zag. Long flat or falling stretches are normal, not a sign the rule stopped working.

The bands are percentiles at each trade separately: the 5% line is not one path, and no single path follows the median line. The vertical range is set by the 5–95% band, so a sample path that strays further can run off the top or bottom of the chart.

14

Why more risk is not more growth

The Lab’s median comes from compounding. With the model of chapter 5, the typical (median) path grows per trade by about

g = p × ln(1 + risk × R:R) + (1 − p) × ln(1 − risk)
p = win rate. The median final equity is about eg × trades. This is the arithmetic behind the figure; the Lab itself does not show g.

The peak sits at risk = p − (1 − p) ÷ R:R (the Kelly fraction): 0.45 − 0.55 ÷ 2 = 17.5% here. It is a ceiling, not a target. Near it the drawdowns are enormous (the Lab shows a median max DD above 90% at 20% risk), and it is only the peak if the win rate and R:R are exactly right; with a win rate a little lower the peak moves left and the same risk lands on the falling side.

Where traders usually sit

The shaded strip (0.5–2%) is far to the left of the peak. There, growth is a little lower than it could be in theory, and the drawdowns are a fraction of the size. That trade is what the Lab lets you see in numbers.

C. A coin flip at 1:1: 50% win, 1% risk, 200 trades

Expectancy 0.50 × 1 − 0.50 = 0.00R. Median result about −1%, profitable paths about 47%, 5th pct about −21%, median max DD about −14.5%, P(DD > 20%) about 22%. No edge, and still a 1-in-5 chance of a 20% drawdown: risk without reward.

D. Just under break-even: 33% win, 2:1, 1% risk

Break-even at 2:1 is 33.3%, so 0.33 × 2 − 0.67 = −0.01R. The Lab shows a median of about −4% and profitable paths about 41%. One point of win rate (or a little of every win lost to costs) is the whole difference between a small edge and none.

Try it: exact odds and streaks

The final-equity figures of the Lab can be computed exactly, without random paths: the final equity depends only on how many trades won, and that count follows a binomial distribution. This calculator gives the values the 1,000 paths estimate, plus the streak arithmetic of chapter 11. Drawdown and ruin depend on the order of trades and need the stress test above.

18

Limits and caveats

Your inputs are estimates

The output is only as good as the win rate and R:R you give it. From a few dozen trades both are uncertain; stress them (chapter 13).

Fixed wins and losses

Every win pays exactly R:R and every loss exactly 1R. Real trades vary; a loss can exceed 1R through a gap or slippage.

No costs

Spread, commission and swap are not modelled. Lower the R:R (or the win rate) to account for them.

Independent trades

Each trade is drawn on its own. Real losses cluster: correlated positions, a market regime that stops suiting the rule, a bad week of decisions.

Constant percentage risk

Risk is a fixed share of current equity, so positions shrink after losses and grow after wins. A fixed dollar risk would give deeper drawdowns in % terms after losses.

Ruin is a line, not the end

“Ruin” is defined as falling below −50%. Accounts can of course lose more; margin calls and stop-outs are not modelled.

Random each run

1,000 paths leave sampling noise of about a point on most percentages; tiny differences between settings mean nothing.

Not a forecast

The Lab describes what given odds imply. It does not know the market and is not financial advice.

20

Glossary

Monte Carlo
Answering a question about chance by playing it out many times with random numbers and looking at the spread of results.
Path
One random sequence of wins and losses, and the equity it produces trade by trade.
R
One unit of risk: what a trade loses if its stop is hit. A +2R trade made twice its risk.
Reward : risk (R:R)
The average win divided by the average loss, both in R.
Expectancy
The average result per trade, in R. Above zero is an edge.
Break-even win rate
The win rate at which expectancy is zero: 1 ÷ (1 + R:R).
Percentile
The value below which a given share of results falls: the 5th percentile is worse than 95% of paths.
Median
The 50th percentile: the middle result.
Drawdown
A fall from the highest equity reached so far, as a share of that peak.
Risk of ruin
Here: the chance of falling below half the starting balance at any point.
Compounding
Risking a share of current equity, so each result changes the size of the next.
Volatility drag
The loss from compounding swings: +x then −x leaves less than you started with.
Kelly fraction
The risk per trade that maximises median growth for given odds: p − (1 − p) ÷ R:R. A theoretical ceiling.
